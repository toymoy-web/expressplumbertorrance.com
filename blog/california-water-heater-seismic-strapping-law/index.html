What California's Water Heater Seismic Strapping Law Actually Requires

It's not a suggestion. Here's what the law covers, what code actually specifies, and what it means for a Torrance homeowner replacing a water heater.

If you've had a water heater replaced in California, you've probably seen the two metal straps wrapped around the tank and wondered whether they're really necessary or just an upsell. They're not optional, and the requirement isn't new. Here's what the law actually says and why it exists.

The legal basis

California Health and Safety Code Section 19211 requires that all new, replacement, and existing residential water heaters be braced, anchored, or strapped to resist falling or horizontal displacement during earthquake motion. The requirement applies to every water heater in the state, not just ones in higher-risk seismic zones, and covers existing units, not only new installations.

The law also requires that anyone selling real property containing a water heater certify in writing that this requirement has been met, which is why water heater strapping shows up in real estate transaction disclosures.

Where the requirement comes from

The current strapping standard was refined after the 1994 Northridge earthquake, when the existing approach to securing water heaters proved insufficient in a number of real failures. An unsecured or improperly secured tank can tip over during strong shaking, which risks both physical injury and, for a gas unit, a fire or gas leak if the connections rupture.

What the actual code requires

The California Plumbing Code, which implements the Health and Safety Code requirement in technical detail, specifies two metal straps at minimum: one positioned in the upper third of the tank's height and one in the lower third, with the lower strap kept at least four inches above the tank's controls, meaning the gas valve or thermostat, so the strap itself can't interfere with or damage them. Each strap needs to wrap fully around the tank and anchor into the wall framing, not just rest against drywall.

Flexible, code-listed connectors for both the water and, on a gas unit, the gas supply line are also part of a properly seismic-secured installation, since a rigid connection straight to the tank is a likely failure point if the tank shifts even slightly during an earthquake.

What this means during a replacement

Every water heater replacement in Torrance should include proper seismic strapping as a standard part of the installation, not an optional add-on. A plumbing permit is also generally required for water heater replacement in California jurisdictions, including Torrance, and pulling that permit is part of what confirms the installation was done to code, strapping included.

If you're looking at an existing water heater in a home you're buying, or one that's been in place for years without ever being checked, it's worth confirming directly whether it's properly strapped rather than assuming. A single strap, a strap resting on drywall instead of anchored into framing, or no strapping at all are all real, common issues found during inspections.

The bottom line

Seismic strapping isn't upsell language from a plumber trying to pad an invoice. It's a specific, code-mandated safety requirement with a clear technical standard behind it, and it should be included as a matter of course in any legitimate water heater installation in California, not billed as an extra.

Other code requirements that come with a water heater installation

Seismic strapping is the most commonly discussed requirement, but it's not the only one. A compliant installation also needs a properly sized temperature-and-pressure relief (TPR) valve with discharge piping that terminates 6 to 24 inches above the ground or a suitable drain, never capped off or piped somewhere unsafe. Garage installations generally require the unit's ignition source to sit at least 18 inches above the floor unless the unit uses flammable-vapor-ignition-resistant technology, which most current gas units already include. A drain pan is typically required for installations in an attic or any location where a leak could cause damage to living space below.

What happens if a water heater isn't properly strapped

Beyond the safety risk itself, an improperly secured water heater can create real problems during a real estate transaction, since the law requires written certification that the strapping requirement has been met. A home inspection that flags missing or improper strapping is one of the more common, and most inexpensive to fix, issues found during a sale. Fixing it is quick and doesn't require replacing the water heater itself, just properly installing code-compliant straps, but it does need to be done correctly, anchored into wall framing, not just resting against drywall, and positioned in the correct upper and lower thirds of the tank.

What to check if you're not sure about your own water heater

If you haven't had your water heater serviced or inspected in years, it's worth a direct visual check: are there two straps, positioned in the upper and lower third of the tank, wrapped fully around it and anchored to the wall studs rather than just drywall? Is the lower strap at least four inches above the gas valve or thermostat controls? If any of that is unclear or looks off, having a licensed plumber verify it during a routine service call is a small, worthwhile step, especially given how directly it ties to both safety and legal compliance.

It's also worth checking the water and gas connectors themselves while you're at it. Code favors flexible, code-listed connectors over rigid pipe run straight into the tank, since a rigid connection is far more likely to crack or separate if the tank shifts even slightly during shaking, straps or no straps. A licensed plumber checking your strapping can confirm connector type at the same time, at no meaningfully added cost to a routine visit.
