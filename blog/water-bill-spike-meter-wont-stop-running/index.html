Why Your Water Bill Spiked and the Meter Won't Stop Running

If your usage habits haven't changed but your bill has, and your meter keeps moving with everything off, here's what's actually going on and how to find it.

An unexplained jump in your Torrance Municipal Water or Cal Water bill, without any real change in how you're using water, is one of the most common reasons people first suspect a hidden leak. Unlike a burst pipe or an obvious puddle, this kind of leak often doesn't announce itself with anything visible, which is exactly why it's worth taking seriously rather than assuming it'll resolve on its own.

The simplest way to confirm a leak exists

Before assuming anything, do a basic meter test: make sure no water is running anywhere in the house, no faucets, no appliances, nobody flushing, and check your water meter. Most meters have a small triangular or circular leak indicator dial that moves even with the tiniest flow. If that indicator is spinning, or if you check the main meter reading, wait 15 to 30 minutes with everything off, and check it again to find it's moved, water is escaping somewhere in the system between the meter and your fixtures.

Common causes, from most to least common

A running toilet

This is the single most common cause of a mysteriously high water bill, and often the easiest to miss, since a toilet that's silently leaking from tank to bowl doesn't always make an obvious running sound. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing; if color appears in the bowl, water is leaking past the flapper valve.

An irrigation system leak

A cracked irrigation line or a valve that isn't fully closing can waste a significant amount of water, especially since irrigation systems often run at times nobody's around to notice a problem, like the middle of the night.

A slab leak

Water escaping from a line running under your home's concrete slab won't be visible until it's caused enough damage to show up as a warm spot on the floor or water at the edge of a wall. This is one of the more serious causes on this list, both because it can go undetected longest and because of the potential for structural impact if left unaddressed.

A water heater leak

A tank slowly leaking, sometimes internally in a way that isn't visible from outside the unit, can account for water loss without any obvious sign at the water heater itself.

A main service line leak

The underground line between the meter and your house can develop a leak the same way any aging pipe can, and because it's buried, it often shows up first as a consistently damp patch of lawn along its likely path, or simply as an unexplained bill increase with no other symptom at all.

Why "the meter must be wrong" usually isn't the answer

It's a natural first instinct to suspect the water utility's meter is malfunctioning or misreading, but meter errors that would produce a sustained, significant bill increase are genuinely uncommon. If your own meter test confirms movement with everything off, that's strong, direct evidence the issue is on your side of the meter, in your home's own plumbing, rather than a billing or utility-side error.

How a professional narrows it down from here

Once a leak is confirmed but the source isn't obvious, electronic and acoustic leak detection equipment can trace the sound of pressurized water escaping, whether that's under a slab, along a service line, or somewhere less obvious like inside a wall. This doesn't require guessing where to start digging or cutting; it narrows the search to a specific, confirmed location before any repair work begins.

What to do while you're waiting to get it checked

If you've confirmed a leak but haven't yet had it located and fixed, it's worth checking your home's main shutoff valve location now, before you need it in a hurry, and monitoring whether the water bill continues climbing or has plateaued, which can sometimes hint at whether the leak is worsening. Beyond that, there's not much to safely do yourself beyond the basic toilet and visible-fixture checks; anything underground or behind a wall genuinely needs detection equipment rather than guesswork.

How much water loss actually translates to dollars

It's worth understanding why even a seemingly minor leak shows up as a real bill increase. A supply-line leak too small to see or hear can still run continuously, 24 hours a day, every day, until it's found, which adds up to a genuinely large total volume over even a single billing cycle. This is different from a dripping faucet, which most people can hear and address quickly; an underground or behind-the-wall leak just keeps running unnoticed, which is exactly why the bill, rather than any visible sign, is often the first real clue something is wrong.

Comparing bills the right way

A single unusually high bill isn't always conclusive on its own, since seasonal irrigation changes or a houseguest staying for a week can shift a bill too. What's more telling is comparing the same billing period year over year, your July bill this year against last year's July bill, for instance, rather than comparing consecutive months against each other. A jump that holds up against last year's same-season comparison, with no corresponding change in your actual household habits, is a stronger, more reliable signal that something in the plumbing itself has changed, not just seasonal water use.

Should you call the water utility or a plumber first?

If your own meter test confirms movement with everything off, the issue is on your side of the meter, in your home's own plumbing or service line, which means a plumber, not the utility, is who actually finds and fixes it. The water utility can confirm your meter itself is functioning correctly if you have genuine doubts about it, but they won't diagnose or repair an in-home or service-line leak, since that's private property, not their infrastructure. Calling a plumber directly, once you've done the basic meter test yourself, is the more direct path to an actual resolution.
