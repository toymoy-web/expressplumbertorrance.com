Sump Pump Checklist Before the South Bay's Rainy Season

A few minutes of testing now beats discovering a dead pump in the middle of a storm. Here's what to actually check.

Torrance doesn't get the sustained, heavy rainfall some parts of the country deal with, but the South Bay's rainy season, generally running from late fall through early spring, still brings the kind of concentrated storms that overwhelm a sump pump nobody's checked since last year. A pump that's been sitting idle for months is exactly the kind of thing that fails silently, only revealing the problem when you actually need it.

Why this matters more than it might seem

The City of Torrance's own storm drain system carries most street runoff directly to the ocean, and the city maintains 17 stormwater retention basins to manage heavier flows, but low-lying areas, including the vicinity of the Madrona Marsh nature preserve, still carry real, documented flood risk during major storms. For a property with a basement, crawlspace, or any below-grade area, a working sump pump is the difference between a heavy storm being a non-event and it becoming a genuine water-damage claim.

The pre-season checklist

1. Test the float switch

Pour a bucket of water into the sump pit, enough to raise the water level to where the float switch should trigger, and confirm the pump activates and drains the water down to its normal resting level. If it doesn't activate, or activates but doesn't fully drain the pit, that's worth addressing before you actually need the pump during a storm, not after.

2. Check the discharge line

Follow the discharge line outside and confirm it's clear of debris, leaves, dirt, anything that could have accumulated over the dry months, and that it discharges far enough from the foundation that pumped water doesn't just flow back toward the house. A clogged or blocked discharge line can make a perfectly functional pump look like it's failed, since the water has nowhere to actually go.

3. Listen for unusual sounds

A pump that runs with grinding, rattling, or straining sounds, rather than a normal, even hum, may have debris caught in the impeller or a motor nearing the end of its service life. Catching this before storm season means a scheduled repair rather than a failure during active rain.

4. Check the pump's age

Most sump pumps have a working life of 7 to 10 years. If you don't know how old yours is, or know it's approaching or past that range, this is a reasonable point to consider proactive replacement rather than waiting for a failure to force the decision at the worst possible time.

5. Consider a battery backup

Heavy storms and power outages tend to arrive together, which is exactly when a standard sump pump, dependent on grid power, is most likely to be needed and least likely to have power. A battery backup system keeps the pump running through an outage, and for a property in a genuinely flood-risk area, it's worth treating as a real part of the system rather than an optional extra.

6. Clear the area around the pit

Make sure nothing has been stored or stacked in a way that would block access to the sump pit or interfere with the float switch's range of motion. This is an easy thing to overlook if the pit is in a storage area that sees other household items accumulate around it over the year.

What to do if you find a problem

If any of these checks turns up an issue, a weak or non-responsive float switch, a grinding sound, a pump clearly past its expected lifespan, it's worth having it looked at before the first serious storm rather than during one. A proactive repair or replacement is a scheduled, routine visit. The same failure discovered mid-storm, with water already accumulating, is treated as the genuine emergency it is, and emergency timing is never the ideal moment to be sourcing a replacement pump.

Primary vs. backup pumps: do you need both?

Most residential setups rely on a single primary pump, powered by household electricity, which handles normal storm loads reliably as long as it's functioning and has power. A secondary or backup pump, whether battery-powered or, in some setups, water-powered, exists specifically for the scenario where the primary fails or loses power during the exact storm that's generating the water it needs to move. For a Torrance property with genuine flood exposure, particularly anything near the lower-lying areas around Madrona Marsh, a backup system isn't excessive caution, it's addressing the realistic scenario where storm and power outage arrive together.

A simple pre-season timeline

A practical approach is running through this full checklist once in early fall, before the rainy season typically begins, and doing a quick float-switch test again after any unusually long dry stretch, since a pump that sat completely idle for months is more likely to have an issue surface than one that's been tested and run periodically. This doesn't need to be complicated: a few minutes with a bucket of water, twice a year, catches the overwhelming majority of problems before they become an emergency.

What a professional inspection adds beyond the DIY checklist

The checklist above catches the most common, easily visible issues, but a professional inspection can go further: testing the pump's actual pumping capacity against its rated specification, checking the check valve on the discharge line (which prevents pumped water from flowing back into the pit between cycles), and assessing whether your current pump is even correctly sized for your property's actual water intrusion risk. For a property that's had water intrusion before, or sits in one of Torrance's more flood-exposed low-lying areas, this deeper check is worth doing at least once, even if the basic DIY test comes back fine.
