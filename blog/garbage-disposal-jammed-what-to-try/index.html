Garbage Disposal Jammed? What to Try Before You Call a Plumber

A humming, silent, or leaking disposal doesn't always need a service call. Here's what's safe to check yourself first, and when to actually stop and call.

A garbage disposal that's stopped working is one of the more common kitchen frustrations, and one of the plumbing issues most homeowners can safely take a first pass at themselves before deciding whether it actually needs a professional. Here's a practical, safe order of operations.

Before you do anything: turn it off and unplug it

This isn't optional. Before reaching anywhere near the disposal's blades or interior, switch it off at the wall switch and, if it's plugged into an outlet under the sink rather than hardwired, unplug it directly. Never put your hand inside a disposal that's still connected to power, regardless of how confident you are that it's off.

Problem: it hums but doesn't spin

This almost always means something is jamming the flywheel, a piece of food, a utensil, or debris that's fallen in. Most disposals have a small hex-shaped opening at the bottom center of the unit, accessible from underneath, designed for exactly this situation. Insert an Allen wrench (many disposals come with one specifically for this) into that opening and manually rotate it back and forth to work the flywheel free. Once it turns freely by hand, restore power and try running it again.

If your model doesn't have this manual turning option, or you can't locate it, a wooden spoon or dowel braced against one of the visible impeller blades from the top, with the power off, can sometimes free a minor jam, but this requires real caution and isn't the recommended approach for anyone not fully confident in what they're doing.

Problem: nothing happens at all, no sound

Check the reset button first, a small red or black button on the bottom of the unit that trips when the motor overheats or draws too much current. Press it firmly; if it clicks back into place, try running the disposal again. If the reset button doesn't resolve it, check whether the unit is receiving power at all, a tripped circuit breaker or a loose plug connection under the sink is a common, easily fixed cause that has nothing to do with the disposal itself.

Problem: leaking from underneath

First, figure out where the leak is actually coming from, since not everything under a sink is the disposal's fault. Check the dishwasher drain hose connection and the sink flange (where the disposal meets the sink drain) before assuming the disposal's internal seals have failed. If the leak is clearly coming from the disposal unit's body itself, rather than a connection point, that typically means an internal seal has failed, which generally means replacement rather than a repairable fix.

Problem: unusual grinding or rattling noise

With the power off, look inside (using a flashlight, never your hand) for anything that shouldn't be there, a piece of glass, metal, or a fruit pit are common culprits. Never reach in with your fingers; use tongs or pliers to remove anything visible. If you don't see an obvious foreign object and the noise persists, it's more likely a worn or damaged component that needs professional attention.

What not to do

Don't pour chemical drain cleaners into a jammed or non-working disposal, they won't address a mechanical jam and can make an eventual professional repair more hazardous to work on. Don't repeatedly flip the switch on and off hoping it'll eventually catch, this can further strain or damage a motor that's already struggling. And don't ever put your hand inside the unit, regardless of how the power switch is positioned, unless it's fully unplugged at the outlet.

When to actually call a plumber

If the manual flywheel turn doesn't free a jam, if the reset button and power checks don't restore function, if there's a confirmed leak from the unit body itself, or if you're just not comfortable doing any of the above yourself, that's the point to call rather than keep troubleshooting. A disposal that's genuinely failed, rather than just jammed, is usually more cost-effective to replace than repair, and a plumber can make that call quickly once they've actually inspected it.

How long should a disposal actually last?

Most residential garbage disposals last somewhere between 8 and 12 years with normal household use, though this varies with how heavily it's used and what tends to go down it. A disposal that's failed well before that range, especially with a confirmed leak from the housing itself rather than a connection point, is a reasonable candidate for straightforward replacement rather than a repair that may not hold for long given the unit's overall condition.

Preventing the next jam

A few habits genuinely reduce how often a disposal jams in the first place: running cold water, not hot, while it's operating, since cold water keeps grease solid enough to be chopped rather than letting it coat the blades and housing; avoiding fibrous foods like celery, corn husks, and onion skins, which tend to wrap around the impeller rather than grind cleanly; and never using a disposal as general trash disposal for anything not genuinely food waste. None of this guarantees a jam-free disposal forever, but it meaningfully reduces how often you're dealing with this particular kitchen frustration.

A note on odor without an obvious jam

If your disposal is running fine mechanically but has developed a persistent smell, that's usually trapped food residue in a spot the water flow doesn't fully clear rather than anything wrong with the unit itself. Running ice cubes through it can help dislodge some buildup, and periodically running it with cold water and a small amount of dish soap helps rinse the housing more thoroughly than water alone. If the smell persists after that, it's worth a professional look, since a genuinely worn unit can develop cracks or gaps that trap debris in ways a simple cleaning routine can't reach.
